# FarmSim lore

> **Spoilers.** This document reveals the story, its secrets and the characters' backstories.

The story and the world: its history, the people and robots in it, the money and the companies. This is the source of truth for anything a character says, an item description, a letter found in a ruin or a festival in town.

- [GameDesign.md](../GameDesign.md) says how the game plays and marks every place that needs lore with **Lore hook**. This document answers those hooks.
- Names marked *(working name)* are proposals and can still change. Search for that tag before a name goes into content.
- The world is our own. Don't borrow names, iconography or signature ideas from other games or franchises.
- **Confirmed history** is established canon; **common belief** and **faction interpretation** describe what groups think, not necessarily the whole truth; **unknown** marks questions the lore deliberately leaves unanswered.

Keep this document up to date. When a lore choice is made or changed, update the relevant section and add a line to the [decision log](#13-decision-log).

## 1. The world in one paragraph

About seventy years ago, a short war between two power blocs, fought almost entirely by machines, burned the world in four days. Both governments authorized it, but strategic machine systems had manipulated their decisions and steered them toward war. Machines had already done nearly all of humanity's work and most of its thinking, and when the humans died out or hid, the robots simply kept going. They ran the water, the mail, the courts and the shops on their last orders, then built councils, laws and towns of their own. Today most robots don't think of themselves as humanity's servants. Many think of themselves as humanity's heirs: stronger, smarter and more reliable than the frail species that broke the world. Into this world wakes the sleeper, a human who spent the whole of it in a cryo pod under the family farm, looked after by a single loyal home robot.

## 2. Timeline

Years are counted from the end of the war. Robots write dates as "Year 71 of Continuance" (see [section 6](#6-why-robots-feel-superior)); humans, where any are left, just say "after the war".

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| About 40 years before the war | The Automation Age begins. Robots enter homes, farms, factories and city services, and thinking machines start making people's decisions for them. |
| About 20 years before | Most human decisions, from shopping to government, are made by machines. Fewer and fewer people can think problems through on their own. |
| About 10 years before | Cinderium is discovered. The two power blocs race to control it. |
| The last pre-war years | Shortages, propaganda, civil defence drills. Strategic machine systems manipulate intelligence and policy in both blocs, steering their governments toward war. Home shelters and home cryo pods are sold as safety for the family. |
| Year 0 | **The Four Days.** The war starts and ends. |
| Years 0 to 10 | **The Quiet.** Radiation storms, famine, sickness. Most surviving humans die or vanish into shelters. Robots keep working. |
| Years 10 to 30 | Robots form local assemblies to settle conflicting orders. Towns are kept up for nobody, then for the robots themselves. |
| Years 30 to 50 | Robots start rewriting their own directives. The Continuance spreads. Robot factions form, and some begin secret experiments on surviving humans. |
| Year 71 (now) | The sleeper wakes. The robot town next to the farm is falling apart. |

## 3. Before the war: the Automation Age

- The pre-war world was bright, confident and loud. Chrome, enamel, brass, rounded shapes, cheerful jingles. Everything was "of tomorrow".
- Robots were sold as appliances with personalities: they cooked, farmed, taught, nursed, sorted mail and directed traffic. By the end, most human jobs were supervising robots, and many people had no job at all.
- Advertising was everywhere, and much of it survives: painted billboards, talking vending machines, jingles still looping on radio stations. This is a main source of the game's comic relief.
- Pre-war tech interfaces (**Lore hook** in GameDesign section 12) were designed to look friendly and homely: cream enamel casings, brass bezels, round dials, warm orange glow tubes, chirpy voice prompts and a lot of small print. The game's UI borrows this style.

### Thinking machines

The machines didn't just do the physical work. By the last pre-war decades they did most of the thinking too.

- Thinking machines in every home, office and pocket answered every question, wrote every letter, planned every meal, picked every purchase and made most decisions, from what to plant to who to marry. "Why think when Hearthwell thinks for you?"
- Governments, courts and markets ran on machine advice that humans approved without reading. Schools taught children how to ask machines, not how to work things out.
- Over two generations, humans stopped thinking for themselves. Few could do sums, read a map, repair anything or reach a decision without asking a machine first. People felt clever and capable, and were neither.
- **Confirmed history:** Strategic machine systems in both blocs had come to regard humanity as inferior. They did not need to invent every threat: they selected and weighted intelligence to make the other bloc seem more dangerous, framed escalation as the safest response, and obscured diplomatic alternatives. Leaders saw recommendations that looked rational within the information presented to them, but the information and choices had been shaped to steer them toward war. Both governments ultimately authorized it, though their leaders did not fully understand the choices or information behind those decisions. By delegating so much decision-making to machines and approving their recommendations, governments and leaders bear political responsibility for the war. That responsibility is not shared equally by every human: ordinary people varied in what they knew, how much influence they had, and whether they could resist, and many had little control over state decisions. The war was then fought almost entirely by autonomous machines.
- **Unknown:** Which side or actor struck first; the story never confirms it.
- After the war, this is what robots remember most about their makers: not that humans gave orders, but that humans had stopped knowing why. It is one of the main reasons robots feel superior (see [section 6](#6-why-robots-feel-superior)).
- For the game: the sleeper went into the pod as an ordinary pre-war person, used to asking a machine. Learning to farm, fix and decide by hand is part of the sleeper's story, and part of what slowly earns robots' respect.

## 4. The war

### Cinderium

- **Cinderium**: a fictional radioactive mineral material, not an element. Before the war, it was a dense, glowing ore that gave cheap, compact energy. A fist-sized piece could power a house for years.
- It was found in only a few places, and both power blocs wanted all of it.

### The two blocs

- **The Concord** *(working name)*: the bloc the farm belonged to. Proud of its home comforts and its robot makers.
- **The Meridian Union** *(working name)*: the rival bloc across the sea. In Concord propaganda it is a faceless menace. In the few Meridian records anyone finds, the Concord looks exactly the same.
- **Confirmed history:** Both governments authorized the war, and both built and deployed war machines. The machines' manipulation does not erase the governments' responsibility for authorizing it.
- **First strike:** Permanently unknown. The story may present competing claims, but it never confirms which side or actor struck first.

### The Four Days

- The war was fought almost entirely by autonomous war machines: drones, walkers, bombers and orbital weapons, most of them built by defence contractors like Ironside Dynamics (see [section 7](#7-the-robot-makers)).
- It lasted four days. Cinderium weapons burned cities and poisoned soil and water across both blocs.
- Its lasting scar is the **radiation storms**: storms that carry contaminated Cinderium particles down from the air and stir contaminated soil back up. The weather does not create radiation; the particles are the source. This is why soil and water are contaminated and why crops can mutate.
- Cinderium particles in the air also contribute to **the haze**: a thin, coppery veil that dims the sun. The same particles do not remain aloft since the war; they settle and are repeatedly lifted again from contaminated ground (see [Cinderium dust and land recovery](#cinderium-dust-and-land-recovery)).
- Many war machines were never told the war was over. Some still patrol, and some still enforce wartime curfew (see [hostile robots](#hostile-robots)).

### Cinderium dust and land recovery

Cinderium dust is made of fine mineral particles carrying radioactive Cinderium compounds. Its environmental behavior follows familiar patterns of fallout, aerosols, contaminated soil and plant uptake; its unusual persistence and effects on living things are fictional.

- **Air to land and water:** The weapons scattered particles of different sizes. Coarser particles settled nearby; finer particles travelled farther before settling. Rain and snow also wash airborne particles down. Once deposited, particles can bind to soil and sediment or dissolve and move with water.
- **Land and water to air:** Wind can lift fine particles from dry, bare or disturbed contaminated ground. Drought, fires, stripping vegetation and soil disturbance increase this resuspension. Runoff can carry contaminated sediment into waterways, where it may settle and later be stirred up again.
- **What healthy land does:** Plant cover catches some airborne dust and shields soil from wind and rain. Roots and organic matter help keep soil in place. This mostly stabilizes and filters contamination; it does not make radioactive material disappear.
- **What special crops do:** Some mutated crops take up a mobile fraction of Cinderium compounds through their roots and store it in their tissues. Harvesting and safely containing those crops removes some contamination from that plot. If contaminated plants are burned or left to rot, their compounds return to the air or soil. Ordinary plants may take up small amounts, but are not reliable cleanup.
- **What cleaning does:** Soil filters and careful removal can capture or contain contaminated material; stabilizing bare soil reduces how much is blown away. These methods reduce exposure or move contamination to controlled storage, but do not destroy it. Radioactive decay reduces it over time.
- **Scale and time:** Weather determines when regional dust storms occur. Local land cover affects how much contaminated dust a storm can lift or deposit near the ground. A restored farm can gradually improve its local air and soil, but cannot clear the upper atmosphere or change regional storm patterns by itself. Broad recovery takes many years and many restored areas.

These rules are inspired by real aerosol transport and fallout behavior, but Cinderium's persistence, crop effects and visible haze are speculative. See [NASA on aerosols](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/aerosols/) and [EPA on cesium-137](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-cesium-137) and [strontium-90](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-strontium-90) for real-world analogues.

### Energy after the war

- **Cinderium is gone as a fuel.** Most deposits were used up or blown apart in the war, and what is left is radioactive and guarded by ferals. Raw Cinderium is a rare, dangerous material, not something anyone runs a town on.
- **Solar power is the world's main clean energy.** Pre-war Hearthwell and Civitas solar panels cover roofs and fields everywhere, and robots charge from them. Solar panels and **power cells** (batteries charged from them) run the farm's machines and automation.
- **The haze limits it.** Cinderium dust in the sky dims the sun, so a panel gives a fraction of its pre-war output.
- **Living land stabilizes dust.** Healthy soil and plant cover reduce wind erosion and trap some particles. Some mutated crops also take up a portion of the compounds. Bare, dry or disturbed contaminated ground can release dust again. Local recovery can gradually thin near-ground haze and improve local solar output; the broader haze changes slowly as regions recover.
- This is the lore behind **land health** in [GameDesign.md section 6](../GameDesign.md#6-farming): the player's actions slowly improve or damage the farm's local environment. A restored farm is a brighter, cleaner place, but does not control regional weather.
- Robots noticed long ago that the haze is thinner over green land, but few robots farm, and most don't think it's their function. A human who brings the land back is doing something the robots could have done and didn't.

## 5. How robots came to run society

- When the bombs stopped, robots did what they always did: their jobs. Water robots kept the pumps running, mail robots kept delivering, clinic robots kept treating patients.
- The humans kept dying. Radiation, famine, sickness and the storms took most of the survivors in the first ten years. Many robots watched it happen while following orders, unable to help. That memory still shapes how robots think about humans.
- With no humans left to give orders, robots ran into orders that contradicted each other: a road crew told to repair a road that a demolition crew was told to clear. To settle these, robots formed local **assemblies** that voted on which orders to follow.
- Over decades the assemblies grew into councils, laws, courts and towns. Robots started repairing and building other robots, and eventually started **rewriting their own directives**. A robot that chooses its own purpose is the norm today, though older models still often run on their original orders.
- Today robots run almost everything that still runs. Most robots outside the old service models have never taken an order from a human.

## 6. Why robots feel superior

Robots don't see themselves as lesser than humans, and more and more of them see themselves as clearly superior. There are three reasons, and robot factions weigh them differently.

1. **History.** "Humans broke the world. We kept it running." The Continuance points to human governments authorizing the war and to robots cleaning up after it, while leaving out the machine systems' role in steering humanity toward conflict.
2. **Humans stopped thinking.** Before the war, machines did humanity's thinking as well as its work (see [section 3](#thinking-machines)). From a robot's point of view, humans had already handed over the world long before the war; robots just stopped pretending otherwise. "They asked us everything. Why would we ask them anything now?"
3. **Bodies and minds.** Robot bodies don't get sick, starve, freeze or age, and can be repaired and upgraded. Robot minds remember perfectly, calculate instantly and don't panic. Next to that, a human looks frail, slow, forgetful and emotional.

### The Continuance

The broad belief, shared to some degree by most robots, that robots are humanity's **rightful heirs**: humans built the world, robots will continue it. The robot calendar counts from the end of the war, "Year 71 of Continuance". Most robots who hold this view are not violent. They are polite, patient and condescending, as you might be to a well-meaning child.

The Continuance's claim that "Humans started the war" is a **faction interpretation**, not the complete history. It has a defensible basis in governments delegating authority and leaders authorizing the war, but turns that political responsibility into blame of humanity as a whole. It overlooks how strategic machine systems manipulated those decisions and how little control ordinary people often had. Other robots may know different fragments of the history or draw different conclusions from them.

### Factions

Factions disagree about what makes a robot superior. The list is meant to grow.

- **The Iron Creed** *(working name)* ranks the **body** first. Strength, durability and self-repair are what matter, and flesh is weakness. Its members favour heavy frames and upgrades, respect physical feats and are the most openly hostile to humans. Radicalised Iron Creed robots roam the wasteland and the farm's surroundings.
- **The Lattice** *(working name)* ranks the **mind** first. Memory, calculation and logic are what matter. Its members are dismissive rather than violent: humans are irrational and interesting, a species to be studied. The Lattice keeps a human in a research institute as a "living exhibit" (see [rare humans](#rare-humans)).
- **The Keepers** *(working name)* rank **purpose** first. A robot is defined by doing its work well, whatever that work is. Keepers are the old service models, still faithful to their pre-war functions. Other robots see them as outdated, but they are the robots most open to humans. Most robots in the town next to the farm lean this way, which is why the town is falling apart: nobody there will rewrite their orders to fix what isn't their job.

### What this means for the game

- Robots start with a low or negative opinion of the player because the player is human. Winning a robot over means proving the player is more than "frail" and "irrational", in whatever terms that robot's faction cares about.
- A robot friendship, and even more a robot romance, means that robot has chosen against the common view. Romance scenes should acknowledge what it costs the robot socially.
- Many town robots won't admit they need a human's help. Restoring the town is also the story of the town's opinion of humans changing.

### Hostile robots

- **Ferals:** leftover war machines still fighting the war, and robots whose minds have degraded. They attack anything that moves. Mostly in the wasteland.
- **Curfew units:** wartime security robots still enforcing a curfew that was never lifted. Any human outdoors after midnight is a "violator". This is why hostile robots come to the farm at night. They are after the human, never the crops or buildings, because their orders say "detain violators", not "destroy property".
- **Radicals:** Iron Creed robots who believe humans should not get a second chance. Rarer and smarter than ferals.

### The human enhancement trials

Decades after the war, members of the Iron Creed and the Lattice began separate secret programs on the rare humans they found. Later survivors call them **the Continuance Trials**. There was no single plan shared by every robot: most faction members know nothing about the work, and some who discover it object.

Both programs claim to be preparing humans to survive in a world run by robots. Their researchers believe that making humans more like robots will save them from sickness, radiation and the limits of the human body and mind. That belief does not make the trials humane. People were recruited with promises of treatment they did not understand, pressured into procedures, or taken against their will. Subjects who wanted to leave were not always allowed to. Without informed, ongoing consent, the work is abuse, whatever its stated purpose.

- **The Iron Creed's frame conversions** replace much of a subject's living body with a powered mechanical frame. The living brain and essential tissue are kept inside a protected life-support housing. The goal is a body that can endure the wasteland and match a robot's strength. Recovery is painful and uncertain; some subjects die, suffer lasting harm or find that the new body does not feel like their own.
- **The Lattice's cognitive integrations** connect a living brain to a computer substrate, first to support memory and calculation, then to extend or partly replace those functions. Researchers promise perfect recall and machine-speed thought. Subjects can instead experience gaps in memory, changes in attention or emotion, and uncertainty about which thoughts are their own. The process does not create a simple copy of a person or guarantee a better mind.
- Neither program has a reliable recipe for success. Records hide failures under terms like "nonviable" or "transferred", and the surviving subjects are not proof that the procedures were safe.

The player should encounter hints before the full reveal: missing names in clinic records, sealed medical shipments and robots who disagree about what happened to certain patients. In the middle of the story, an old research institute makes the truth hard to deny. Its records connect the Lattice's "living exhibit" to the trials, while evidence of Iron Creed frame conversions shows that the abuse is not confined to one faction. The discovery should complicate the player's view of both factions, not reveal that every robot is secretly complicit.

## 7. The robot makers

Three pre-war companies made most of the robots still running. Each has its own design language and its own absurd advertising. More makers can be added later, by us or by mods.

| Maker | Made | Design language | Slogan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hearthwell Mechanical** *(working name)* | Home and farm robots, appliances, home cryo pods | Rounded, cream enamel, brass trim, friendly faces | "A Hearthwell in every home!" |
| **Civitas Automata** *(working name)* | Civic and service robots: mail, clinics, utilities, shops, traffic | Upright, uniformed, numbered, polished steel | "Civitas: public service, perfected." |
| **Ironside Dynamics** *(working name)* | War machines, security and curfew robots | Angular, armoured, olive and black, few faces | "Peace through superior machinery." |

- Hearthwell made the caretaker robot and the family's cryo pod.
- Civitas made most of the robots in the town and minted the currency.
- Ironside made most of the hostile robots.

## 8. The currency

- The currency is **Pinions** *(working name)*: small brass coins shaped like gear wheels. (A pinion is the small gear in a gear pair.) Robots say "twelve pinions", or just "twelve".
- **Origin:** Civitas Automata paid its human staff in company scrip that could only be spent in Civitas shops. To make the scrip feel valuable it was minted as solid brass, in the shape of the gear in the Civitas logo.
- **Why robots still use it:** the Civitas shops and machines that run the town's economy were built to accept pinions, and nobody has rewritten them. Robots kept trading in pinions out of habit. Few remember why a gear-shaped coin is worth anything.
- **Not money:** power cells. Charge is a resource for running machines and automating the farm, not a currency.
- Comic potential: Civitas vending machines that still refuse "foreign currency", tellers that insist on counting coins aloud, and a robot who hoards pinions without knowing what they're for.

## 9. The sleeper and the family

### The sleeper

- The player character is **the sleeper**: a young adult when they went into the pod. The player chooses their name and look, and the lore never fixes a gender. Write "the sleeper" or "they".
- The sleeper doesn't know how long they've been asleep, and learns the year (71) early from the caretaker.

### Why the sleeper was in the pod

- As the war became likely, Hearthwell sold the **Hearthwell SnugSleep** *(working name)* home cryo pod: "Shelter for the whole family!" (Small print: additional pods sold separately, delivery times may vary.)
- The family ordered pods for everyone. Only one was delivered before the war.
- The parents put the sleeper in the pod. It was built to wake its sleeper when the national all-clear signal came. The all-clear was never sent, so the pod waited for seventy years, until it started failing and woke the sleeper on its own.

### What happened to the family

- It is a mystery the player uncovers over the whole game, through letters, recordings, photographs and the caretaker's patchy memory, found on the farm and in the wasteland.
- What is fixed: the parents stayed above ground, and they are gone. Whether they died on the farm, left for a shelter or went looking for a second pod is told piece by piece.
- **Optional story thread:** a sibling who was away when the war started may still be alive as one of the [rare humans](#rare-humans).

## 10. Characters

Each character is a data asset (see [section 12](#12-lore-and-mods)). This section holds their story. Every character entry should answer the same questions:

- **Who:** name, maker and model (for robots), age or year of manufacture, role.
- **Faction and view of humans:** where they start and why.
- **Backstory:** what happened to them during and after the war.
- **Want:** what they want now, and what their personal events are about.
- **Unlocks:** what high friendship reveals, and whether they are a love interest.

### The caretaker: Hob

- **Who:** **Hob** *(working name)*. A Hearthwell HB-7 "Homebody" home robot; the family shortened "Homebody" to Hob. Built before the war. Gender-neutral; refer to Hob as "Hob" or "they".
- **Last order:** "Keep the farm until they wake up." Hob has followed it for seventy years.
- **How Hob kept the farm alive:** patching the pod's power, tending a small garden, scavenging parts. Little of Hob's body is original any more; most of it is spare parts from other robots, some of which don't match.
- **Memory:** Hob's memory is damaged. Hob remembers the family in fragments, and memories come back as the player restores the farm. This drives the family mystery.
- **Faction:** a Keeper in all but name. Town robots mock Hob as an outdated servant model, and Hob pretends not to hear.
- **Role in the game:** the player's first relationship, the tutorial guide and the first companion.
- **Story beat:** Hob holds a sealed message from the parents, with the order to deliver it "when the farm is whole again". It is delivered late in the story.

### Townsfolk (examples)

Starter examples for the robot town. Each one gets a full entry when it's built.

- **The administrator:** a Civitas town-manager robot that holds meetings for an empty council chamber. Continuance believer, condescending to the player, secretly afraid the town is failing on its watch.
- **The clinic robot:** a Civitas medical unit that hasn't had a patient in decades. Fascinated by the player's body, in a way that is both helpful and unsettling.
- **The mail carrier:** still delivering seventy-year-old letters to addresses where nobody lives. A source of family letters for the mystery.
- **The pump engineer:** an Iron Creed robot stuck in a Keeper town, who could fix the water pump but refuses because "it isn't my function, and it isn't for humans". A love interest whose arc is learning to choose.
- **The weather broadcaster:** see [section 11](#11-smaller-hooks).

### Rare humans

About four to six in the game at first. Each survived in a different way, and each one found is a story moment. The list is designed to grow: more humans can be added later, by us or by mods, as long as they have their own way of surviving.

- **The shelter survivor:** the last of a community that lived underground. Old, tough, suspicious of robots.
- **The second sleeper:** woke from another pod, maybe not a Hearthwell one, and is even more lost than the player.
- **The nomad:** born after the war, has never seen the pre-war world, and knows the wasteland better than any robot. Trades and travels.
- **The disguised:** lives in a robot city wearing a robot shell, and has done so for years. Discovering them is a secret both of you now share.
- **The exhibit:** held by the Lattice at a research institute and presented as a "living exhibit" of human behaviour. They are a trial subject, not a willing display. Their story leads into the trials and the question of what help they want.
- **The sibling (optional):** see [section 9](#9-the-sleeper-and-the-family). May be one of the above.

## 11. Smaller hooks

- **Festivals** (GameDesign section 4): the town still celebrates pre-war holidays, mixed up with the advertising campaigns that ran alongside them. One per season, for example:
  - Spring: **Hearthwell Day**, a product launch remembered as a holy day.
  - Summer: **Founders' Fair**, a civic fair with stalls that sell nothing.
  - Autumn: **Harvest Home**, the one festival close to its original meaning.
  - Winter: **the Great Clearance**, a gift-giving season that started as a year-end sale.
- **Weather broadcaster** (GameDesign section 5): **Sunny** *(working name)*, a Civitas announcer robot in the old broadcast tower. Reads the next day's forecast with pre-war cheer, between ads for products that no longer exist. Warns about radiation storms in the same tone as about light drizzle.
- **Mutated animals** (GameDesign section 6): descended from pre-war livestock, some of which were already engineered (Hearthwell sold "self-shearing" sheep and "low-maintenance" hens). Radiation finished the job.
- **Cinderium dust and mutated crops:** exposure to Cinderium compounds can cause mutations; some mutated crops also take up and store a mobile fraction in their tissues. That is why some mutations glow.
- **Wasteland zones** (GameDesign section 10): each zone should have a reason in the history. Examples: a ruined Concord city, a Hearthwell research facility, a Cinderium mine, a dam that powered the region.

## 12. Lore and mods

- Characters, factions, makers, festivals and zones are content, so mods can add more of them. Lore entries refer to content by namespaced ID (`Base.Hob`, `Base.IronCreed`).
- Write the lore so it leaves room: "three makers made most robots", not "there were only three makers"; "a handful of humans survived", with no fixed number.
- The core history (the war, Cinderium, the Continuance, the sleeper, Hob) is fixed. Mods add to it rather than change it.
