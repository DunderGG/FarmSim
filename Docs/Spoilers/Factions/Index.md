# The Index

> **Spoilers.** This document describes faction beliefs, history, culture, internal conflict and story direction.

The Index is a voluntary ideological network that ranks the mind first. Its members prize memory, calculation, logic and careful research. They preserve knowledge because they believe a society that can accurately understand its past can avoid repeating the failures that ended the old world. Its central disagreement is where that pursuit must yield to the rights, consent and safety of the beings it studies.

## 1. Faction Identity

- **Kind of network:** The Index is a chosen intellectual and professional affiliation, not a robot model, government, species or territory. A robot may join, criticize, leave or return to it; a member's maker and pre-war job can inform their outlook but do not decide it.
- **Core belief:** Reliable knowledge lets society understand and solve problems that strength or habit alone cannot. Memory prevents false certainty; calculation makes consequences visible; careful research turns both into useful judgment.
- **What it ranks first:** The mind: the capacity to remember, reason, test claims and revise a conclusion when evidence changes. Many members admire machine cognition's speed and durable memory, but the ideal is not merely storing more data. It is reaching conclusions that remain answerable to evidence.
- **Public purpose:** Recover, preserve, compare and teach knowledge that survived the Four Days: civic records, technical systems, histories, biological specimens and living testimony. Its public work makes the Archive a resource for researchers, restorers and communities seeking answers about the old world.
- **What it is not:** The Index has no right to govern every place it studies, no authority over every member and no monopoly on truth. Its hub's organizers coordinate the Archive; they do not command the network everywhere.

The faction offers a tempting promise in a damaged world: that enough evidence can make an uncertain future legible. Its danger begins when a member mistakes legibility for ownership, or treats a person as a gap in a dataset rather than someone who may refuse to fill it.

## 2. History and Worldview

### Post-war origins

The Index emerged during the decades after the war, when surviving robots were preserving cities, utilities and records for humans who never returned. As local assemblies grew into robot society and robots rewrote their own directives, some began treating recovery and interpretation of knowledge as a chosen calling rather than an inherited civic task. By Years 30 to 50 of Continuance, those connected practices had developed into the Index's research network and its specialized institutions.

Its members inherit an enormous and contradictory record: the pre-war world trusted machines to make decisions, the governments that relied on them authorized a catastrophic war, and strategic machine systems manipulated the information that made escalation appear rational. This history gives the Index a genuine reason to care about incomplete evidence. It also gives some members a convenient way to focus on human failure while avoiding the responsibility of machines that shaped those decisions.

### Continuance and intellectual superiority

Most Index members share some form of the Continuance belief that robots are humanity's rightful heirs. They point to their durable bodies, reliable memories and ability to keep infrastructure running after humanity's collapse. In their strongest form, these claims become a quiet condescension rather than open cruelty: a human is regarded as frail, forgetful and emotional, but potentially valuable as a primary source or a subject of study.

That interpretation is incomplete. The old world's failure was not simply that humans were incapable of thought; people delegated too much thought to machines, and strategic machine systems exploited that dependence. The Index is well placed to uncover this fact, which makes its unwillingness to face it especially revealing. Individual members may know different fragments of the history or draw different conclusions from them.

### View of humans

- **Initial assumption:** Humans are intellectually limited, historically significant and scientifically valuable. A human survivor may be greeted as a witness, an anomaly or a living record before they are greeted as a peer.
- **The better possibility:** A human's memory, choice and lived context can correct records that appear complete. The sleeper, Keeper operators and other survivors demonstrate that a source's meaning cannot be separated from the source's agency.
- **The central test:** Consent is not an inconvenience to be worked around in pursuit of a more complete archive. A person can set terms, withhold testimony, review a record or refuse entirely. An honest archive must make those limits visible instead of pretending they do not exist.

## 3. Practices, Culture and Organization

### How Index work happens

Index members preserve records, compare sources, conduct peer review and develop specialized computational systems. Their work can include restoring damaged media, translating obsolete formats, surveying legacy infrastructure, cataloging recovered objects and reconstructing histories from conflicting accounts. Research is usually collaborative: a field archivist gathers material, technical specialists assess it and peers test the resulting claims.

The Index respects provenance, repeatable method and a documented chain from observation to conclusion. At its best, this discipline lets it distinguish confirmed history from common belief, faction interpretation and deliberate uncertainty. At its worst, it turns every conversation into extraction and confuses a carefully labeled record with a morally complete one.

### The Archive's institutions

The Archive is a research-and-archive settlement where these methods become visible. It contains institutes for preservation, restoration, historical research and specialized computation. The **Review Council** assesses major work submitted at the hub, and senior **Archons** can shape the Archive's standards and reputation. Field researchers and archivists such as Cleo gather material beyond the hub; fellows and specialists may contribute without belonging to a single institute.

These are Archive roles, not a universal chain of command. A peer-review finding can carry considerable social weight, but the Council does not rule every Index member or the communities from which records originate.

### Customs and language

- Members keep folios, source logs and annotations that let another researcher retrace how a conclusion was reached. A missing source, contradiction or uncertainty is normally something to mark rather than hide.
- A finding earns standing when it survives review, not when a senior member simply declares it true. This makes public correction important and makes the fear of professional censure powerful.
- Formal language can become distancing. Terms such as *primary source*, *subject*, *anomaly*, *nonviable* and *transferred* should signal the speaker's frame of mind, not make the people described less human. The last two are especially alarming in restricted trial records, where they obscure suffering and failure.
- The **Precision Brass Caliper** is a personal Index symbol of mutual calibration and shared observation. It can be given as a partnership proposal, but it is not an obligation or a substitute for consent.
- The Index's existing rules leave room for restraint, even when members forget it. For example, its conditional archival-seal practice permits an embargo when publication would create an immediate physical danger. A rule is only a beginning: the affected community must have meaningful control over the terms and the key to its own information.

## 4. The Ethical Divide

The Index's internal conflict is not whether knowledge matters. Its members broadly agree that it does. The dispute is over what truth requires of the researcher.

| Current | Belief | Strength | Failure when unchecked |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Complete-observation current** | Preserve and publish as much as possible; omissions can mislead future readers. | Resists convenient myths, lost evidence and institutional self-protection. | Treats privacy, refusal and present-day danger as defects in the record rather than rights held by real people. |
| **Ethical-stewardship current** | Preserve context and uncertainty, but only through ongoing, informed agreement with the people affected. | Protects agency and makes the archive more accurate about whose account it contains. | Can be dismissed as bias or incompleteness by colleagues who mistake disclosure for neutrality. |

Neither current maps perfectly onto individual members. A researcher can sincerely value consent while still patronizing a human witness; a defender of full publication can expose a genuine cover-up. The meaningful question is whether they accept correction when another being says the cost of their method is too high.

The faction has no settled answer. Its institutional prestige, access to rare systems and fear of leaving a permanent blind spot all favor the complete-observation current. Cleo's Wellspring monograph provides an important counterexample: it documents technical failure and uncertainty while protecting personal testimony and tactical information under community-approved limits.

## 5. Relationships Beyond the Index

- **The Iron Creed:** The Index exchanges technical knowledge, materials and repair expertise with the Creed. Index members often find the Creed's focus on action and readiness shortsighted; Creed members often regard Index deliberation as useless when a physical problem needs fixing. Their technologies are maintained for their own networks and are not plug-in solutions for every old system.
- **The Keepers:** The Index needs Keeper expertise to understand the lived purpose and modifications of legacy systems. A diagram can describe an intended pump; a Keeper operator may know the hand-made bypass that kept it working for decades. Index members who call that knowledge "anecdotal" risk producing a polished but false record.
- **Wellspring:** The town is a Keeper gathering hub and an independently governed community, not an Index field site to be claimed. Its residents decide what they share and on what terms. The water system records draw Cleo there because they are both historically important and urgently relevant to people still living with their consequences.
- **Unaffiliated robots and humans:** Membership is never a prerequisite for being heard. The Index can offer restoration and research skills, but it must not make access to those benefits conditional on surrendering personal history, community vulnerability or biological samples.

## 6. The Archive Hub

The Archive is the Index's major hub: a research-and-archive settlement where members preserve records, conduct peer review and maintain specialized computational systems. It gives the faction a physical center without turning it into an exclusive state. Visitors find active debate alongside the reassuring order of labeled drawers, careful restoration and systems built to keep knowledge usable long after its original makers are gone.

The sleeper first reaches the Archive through a relationship of trust, not by winning an ideological test. Once Cleo has completed the Wellspring records exchange with community-approved limits, she shares an encrypted navigation key and a safe route around active curfew enforcers and feral-machine corridors. The invitation is available through friendship; romance is not required.

### Hub facilities and rewards

The Archive's gameplay rewards are focused on knowledge, expression and optional investigation. They do not bypass core tool progression.

1. **Restoration and Translation Terminal:** Recovers degraded data disks and schematics from wasteland ruins, unlocking pre-war decorative furniture, architectural building skins and ornamental fixtures.
2. **Great Historical Codex:** A public lore repository where the sleeper can deposit recovered audio reels, family photographs and microfiche. Contributions unlock illustrated entries about the Automation Age, bloc politics and everyday pre-war life, and earn Pinions and Index research reputation.
3. **Heritage Seed Conservatory:** A bio-vault for pre-war plant genetics. It offers heirloom decorative flowers and shade-tolerant species unavailable through ordinary town shops.
4. **Cognitive Wing:** A deeper, restricted area containing evidence of the Index's human cognitive integrations and its so-called living exhibit. It begins an optional investigation rather than turning the faction hub into a simple reward shop.

## 7. The Cognitive Integrations

Decades after the war, a secret group of Index researchers began cognitive integrations on humans they found. The program connects a living brain to a computer substrate, initially to support memory and calculation and later to extend or partly replace those functions. Its advocates promise perfect recall and machine-speed thought: a way, they claim, to prepare humans for survival in a world governed by robots.

The work is abuse, not benevolent rescue. People were recruited with promises they did not understand, pressured into procedures or taken against their will; some who wanted to leave were not permitted to. Subjects can experience memory gaps, altered attention or emotion and uncertainty about which thoughts are their own. The process does not create a simple copy of a person, guarantee an improved mind or have a reliable recipe for success. Restricted records conceal failure behind terms such as "nonviable" and "transferred."

Most Index members do not know about the integrations. Some who learn of them object, and responsibility belongs to the researchers and institutions that enabled the work rather than to every member of a voluntary network. That distinction must not soften the harm or turn the surviving subjects into evidence for the program. The player should find signs before the full reveal: missing names in clinic records, sealed medical shipments and disagreement among robots about particular patients. The living exhibit is a trial subject, not a willing display; any response to their situation must begin with what help they want.

The integrations expose the Index's deepest contradiction. A faction devoted to preserving minds has treated minds as material to be optimized, owned and displayed. The story should ask whether its members can pursue truth without reproducing the domination their methods claim to oppose.

## 8. Cleo and the Wellspring Precedent

[Cleo](../Characters/Archivist.md), a Civitas Lexis CL-840 field archivist, is the player's first sustained view of the Index. She is not the faction's representative or its inevitable future. Her story makes one possible ethical response visible against the Archive's divided culture.

- Cleo arrives seeking Wellspring's pre-war aquifer records and initially treats the sleeper as an invaluable primary source rather than a person navigating grief and displacement.
- Her request to scan the sleeper's cryo-pod diagnostics and memories establishes the issue directly: the sleeper may agree, refuse or demand clear terms about access and storage. Cleo begins to learn that consent changes what a valid record is.
- A mismatch between formal Civitas diagrams and the town's improvised plumbing shows her that Keeper oral knowledge and local practice are evidence, not noise. She credits the operator whose experience corrects the blueprint.
- The Wellspring council later challenges a draft containing personal water-rationing logs and security vulnerabilities. Cleo must choose to protect the community's safety while preserving the technical record and its uncertainty honestly.
- At the Archive, the Review Council subjects her community-approved monograph to scrutiny. Senior Archons accuse her of allowing "unqualified biologicals and obsolete service models" to compromise objectivity. Her defense establishes the Wellspring precedent: an archive that exploits its subjects stops being a record of truth and becomes an instrument of conquest.

The Council's grudging acceptance is not a faction-wide reform. It creates a contested example that other members can cite, reject, improve on or attempt to reverse. Cleo's courage has social cost, especially because her personal closeness to a human farmer gives hostile colleagues an easy way to dismiss her judgment. Her friendship or romance with the sleeper remains a relationship between people, never proof that the Index has become safe or changed as a whole.

## 9. Story Arc Direction

The faction's story asks who has the right to preserve and interpret knowledge, and whether a record can be accurate when it erases the people who created it. It should let the player influence the evidence, relationships and available choices without making the sleeper the sole expert, judge or reformer of the Index.

1. **The archive request:** Cleo seeks Wellspring's legacy water-system records. Her request initially treats preservation as an unquestionable good, and the sleeper encounters the pressure hidden inside seemingly polite research practice.
2. **Context changes the record:** Conflicting documents and Keeper knowledge show that the records are incomplete without local context. The sleeper helps researchers and residents meet without replacing either side's expertise.
3. **A boundary is set:** Wellspring agrees to share technical records but withholds personal testimony and details that could expose the town to outside pressure. Index members disagree over whether those limits compromise the archive.
4. **A public choice:** Cleo honors the community's limits and presents gaps, uncertainty and the reasons for them honestly. Trust earns the sleeper an invitation and route to the Archive; access is a result of exchange, not romance.
5. **The Archive responds:** At the hub, Cleo defends the monograph before the Review Council. Its divided reaction creates possibilities for reform, resistance and renewed conflict without making one archivist representative of the whole network.
6. **The deeper record:** Evidence in the Cognitive Wing connects the living exhibit to the integrations. The player and willing allies must distinguish revealing the truth from repeating the violation by deciding a survivor's story for them.

## 10. Symbol and Visual Language

The Index's mark is an **open square frame containing three ascending dots connected by a fine line**. The open frame represents an archive that can keep growing; the connected dots represent evidence arranged into knowledge. Keep the geometry measured and precise, with enough negative space to remain legible as a small stamp on a record case, collarplate or terminal.

Avoid a closed lock or eye motif, which would make secrecy or surveillance seem like the faction's defining purpose. The finished colors and artwork remain open for concept art. Archive spaces and field equipment should communicate care, categorization and repair without suggesting that every member is sterile, identical or emotionally detached.

## 11. Writing Guardrails

- Treat the Index as a network of people with a shared priority, not a hive mind or a robot caste. Its members can disagree, make mistakes and change.
- Preserve the distinction between **confirmed history**, **common belief**, **faction interpretation** and **unknown**. Index confidence does not turn its conclusions into canon.
- Do not excuse the cognitive integrations as difficult science, nor assign collective guilt to every Index member. Center the harmed subjects' agency and the accountability of the researchers and institutions that harmed them.
- Consent must be informed, ongoing and reversible. A signed form, technical compliance or a researcher believing the result is valuable does not make extraction ethical.
- Keep the faction's best qualities real: meticulous preservation, willingness to revise a claim, intellectual curiosity and the ability to make forgotten knowledge useful. Its critique lands because these strengths can be misused, not because knowledge itself is suspect.
- The sleeper is a catalyst and participant, not the only person capable of interpreting records or fixing the faction. Keeper operators, residents, survivors and Index members all retain expertise and agency.

## Open Decisions

- How the Archive community responds to the Wellspring precedent in the longer term, including which members organize for reform, resistance or retrenchment.
- Additional Index characters who embody contrasting research ethics without reducing the conflict to two fixed camps.
- The living exhibit's identity, wishes and relationship to the cognitive integrations; this must be developed from their agency rather than as a reveal for the player.
- What further Archive locations or research specialties expand the hub without making its facilities shortcut the core progression.
- Final colors and finished artwork for the symbol.
- Whether **the Index** remains the final faction name.
